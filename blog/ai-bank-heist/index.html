One Guy, Seven Banks

In October 2026, a single operator with an AI agent hit seven South Korean financial firms and walked out with about 68,000 people's records. He never touched a bank vault. This is the heist, step by step, and what it teaches about defense.

A visual breakdown. Based on reporting from Oct 6 to 8, 2026. Full sources at the end.

The short version

  • CrowdStrike's assessment, with moderate confidence: likely one Chinese-speaking operator, probably in Maoming, Guangdong. Caught by his own opsec failure: open server directories full of his session logs. (CrowdStrike, via TBS News)
  • The weapon was ARTEX, a free open-source AI pentesting agent, driven by rented models (DeepSeek, GLM, Grok). Built for defense, repurposed for theft. (WSJ, The Decoder)
  • He never touched core banking. Every breach went through side doors: loan-broker sites, employee systems, sales consoles. The AI did the grinding; he did the steering. (TechTimes, AIStockWire)
  • No money moved. The data was the product, headed for Telegram resale groups. (CrowdStrike, via TBS News)

01The heist

The numbers first. Seven financial firms. About 68,000 people's records: names, phone numbers, annual incomes, loan limits, and in 66 cases the national ID number. The biggest single haul, around 40,000 records, came from Yegaram Savings Bank, a name most people outside Korea have never heard.

The method is the story. Nobody cracked a vault or broke the mobile banking app. The attacker went after the side doors: loan-broker websites, employee support systems, sales consoles. The kind of systems every company runs and almost nobody guards like the crown jewels.

And the crew was one person. One laptop, one AI agent, seven banks. The Ocean's Eleven reboot nobody asked for.

03The targets

Seven firms, seven side doors. Tap each card for the entry point and the damage. Notice what is missing from every single one: the front door.

Side door: M Shinhan, a mobile site for loan brokers. The attacker typed values into a loan-application lookup until valid customer numbers came back, then pulled the records attached to them. Names, phone numbers, annual incomes, loan limits. For 66 people, the national ID number too.

Detected after 15h 26m

Side door: the savings bank's customer systems. Public reporting has not detailed the exact entry point here, which is itself the point: the largest haul came from the least-scrutinized target.

The biggest single breach of the campaign

Side door: corporate customer records. Smaller firms, same pattern: real data sitting behind a login page nobody was watching.

Side door: a public page for looking up home-loan brokers. Names, contact details, email addresses, and national ID numbers of 146 mortgage loan solicitors walked out through a directory page.

Side door: a mobile system used by employees, not customers. Staff tools carry the same data with a fraction of the scrutiny.

Detected after 67h 41m, the slowest of the three timed breaches

Side door: a sales support system. Eighty-nine customers' records, taken through the tooling the sales team uses every day.

Detected after 41h 44m

Side door: names and phone numbers of 11 outside developers. No customer data leaked here, but the pattern holds: the edge, not the core.

The front door: locked.

Core banking apps and transaction systems were untouched at all seven firms. Woori Bank and NH NongHyup saw the same attack IPs and blocked them cold. The vault was never the target. It never is.

04The blind spot

These were not smash-and-grab jobs. They were slow, quiet, and long. By the time anyone noticed, the data was gone:

Now the contrast. Three internet banks, Kakao Bank, K Bank, and Toss Bank, saw attack attempts from the same IP addresses and stopped them. Regulators point to two differences: those banks ask for more than an ID and password to get in, and they keep fewer side systems hanging off the open internet. Fewer doors, better locks.

05Why this keeps working

Here is the uncomfortable math. Nothing in this campaign required a zero-day, a lab, or a budget. The tool was free on GitHub. The models were rented by the token. The techniques, credential stuffing and parameter guessing, are older than most of the engineers defending against them. The only new ingredient was an agent that never sleeps, doing the boring work of trying, rotating, and retrying while one person steered.

That is the collapse of heist economics. The crew is now optional. And the defense barely changes: guard the side doors like the front door, require more than a password, watch for bulk reads on peripheral systems, and close your directories. The boring basics, applied to the systems everyone forgot.

The banks were not the only targets. Two Seoul megachurches, Yoido Full Gospel and Sarang, are probing suspected AI-linked intrusions that may have exposed hundreds of thousands of congregants' records. Same pattern, different sector: a side door nobody was guarding.
