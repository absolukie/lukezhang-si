The black hole's rings are a bug

October 5, 2026 · webgl · shaders

The fine vinyl-record striations on my black hole page are a documented WebGL shader artifact — differential rotation winds the disk texture into sub-pixel rings within minutes, and every shader author treats it as a bug to suppress. I decided it looks sick, so I kept it on purpose. Here's everything I learned building it.

1. The bug

Jonas Javier wrote it up while building his own WebGL Gargantua: apply real differential rotation to a noise-textured disk and "within minutes the disk turned into concentric rings thinner than a pixel." The inner disk orbits faster than the outer disk, so any texture gets sheared into ever-finer filaments until it aliases into perfect rings. Shader authors add machinery specifically to kill this.

Real science visualizations don't look like this either. NASA Goddard's famous black hole renders show turbulence sheared into spiral light-and-dark lanes — streaky, not grooved. Interstellar's Gargantua is smooth with a temperature gradient. The vinyl look is pure stylization. Which is exactly why it belongs on my page.

2. How the disk actually moves

The one thing worth getting right is the rotation. A real accretion disk doesn't spin like a record — the inner edge orbits far faster than the rim. Kepler's third law says orbital speed falls off as r^(-3/2), so the shader samples its noise texture in co-rotating material coordinates:

angle = atan(z, x) - t * OMEGA(r)
OMEGA(r) = OMEGA_IN * (rIn / r)^1.5

Each radius of the disk effectively has its own clock. The noise is also sampled in log-radius space, so the banding stays scale-invariant from the blazing inner rim to the faint outer edge. Get this wrong and the disk looks like a spinning sticker; get it right and it feels like orbiting gas even before you add any detail.

One subtlety: perfectly concentric bands can't show rotation at all — they're radially symmetric by construction. So the disk also carries bright knots and spiral streaks sampled purely in the advected angle. Those are what your eye actually tracks as the inner disk laps the outer.

3. The trick that keeps it spinning forever

You can't just let t * OMEGA(r) grow forever — the differential winding accumulates and the rings collapse into aliasing mush, exactly Javier's artifact. The fix, independently documented by multiple shader authors:

  • Run two copies of the disk texture, half a cycle apart (my cycle is 20 seconds).
  • Cross-fade between them with a sine-squared weight.
  • Each layer's weight hits exactly zero at the moment its local time wraps, so the restart is invisible.

Total winding never exceeds one 20-second cycle, so the bands stay crisp forever. A separate flicker octave runs on absolute time (not per-layer time) so it glues the two layers together during the crossfade instead of revealing the seam.

4. The step-budget bug that deleted the sky

The nastiest bug in the whole build wasn't visual — it was architectural. The raymarcher used a fixed step size (dt = 0.14) with 96 steps, for a maximum path length of ~13 units. But the default camera sits 20+ units out. Rays aimed above the shadow exhausted their step budget looping near the photon sphere: never captured, never crossing the disk, just… giving up. The lensed arcs above the shadow — the signature Interstellar look — were never computed at all. The disk read as a separate mound floating under the shadow.

The fix is adaptive steps: dt = clamp(0.09 * r, 0.035, 0.9). Long strides cruise the empty far field where gravity is negligible; fine steps resolve the strong-field lensing near the hole. Same step budget, but the bent rays finally complete their paths — and the arcs appeared. Lesson: when your lighting looks wrong, check whether your rays are even finishing the trip.

5. The branch cut hiding in the texture

There was a sharp diagonal seam across the disk where fine striations abruptly met coarse marbling. Root cause: atan's branch cut. Any angular multiplier that isn't an integer (a * 2.2, a * 3.4…) makes the noise jump discontinuously across the cut — and the cut's screen projection is a diagonal line. The fix: sample all angular noise in periodic coordinates, vec2(cos(a), sin(a)), which are continuous around the full circle, and keep spiral leans at integer values. If your procedural texture has a mysterious straight seam, check your angle math for branch cuts.

6. Making it look hot

Motion is half the sell; the other half is light:

  • Temperature gradient: white-hot inner rim (1.00, 0.96, 0.88) falling off through vivid amber to deep red-orange at the rim.
  • Doppler beaming: the side of the disk rotating toward you gets boosted by roughly pow(g, 3) — more than twice as bright, tinted hot amber-white against the receding side's deep red ember. This is real physics and it's the single biggest contributor to the "that's a black hole" read. (Careful: push the tint too blue and the whole disk goes pastel — I learned that one the ugly way.)
  • Thin photon ring: a tight Gaussian on the shadow's impact parameter, bright but narrow — a thick one reads as a glowing donut.

7. What the math gives you for free

The photon ring and the arcs of disk lensed above and below the shadow aren't painted on anywhere. They fall out of the geodesic light-bending integration itself — march the rays through curved spacetime and the lensing just shows up. That's the best kind of graphics programming: you implement the physics and the beauty is a side effect.
