How fine-tuning works (press the buttons)

October 6, 2026 · machine learning · interactive

Fine-tuning is taking a model that's already smart and teaching it your specific thing: your voice, your task, your data. You don't retrain the whole brain. You nudge a tiny fraction of it with your examples until it behaves the way you want. Below: train a simulated model yourself, see which weights actually change, and watch a model's personality shift.

1. Train it yourself

This is a simulated training run. The line is the model's error (loss): lower is better. Press Train, then drag the learning rate slider and train again. The learning rate is how big each nudge is. Too small and it crawls. Too big and it explodes.

2. What actually changes: almost nothing

Here's the trick that makes fine-tuning cheap. A model's knowledge lives in giant weight matrices (gray grid). In LoRA fine-tuning, you freeze all of that and only train two tiny matrices, A and B (green). The model's update is just A times B added on top. Pick a rank below: bigger rank means more trainable weights, but even the biggest option here is a rounding error next to the full model.

Grid sizes are illustrative (a real matrix is 4096×4096), but the parameter math is real: LoRA with rank r on that matrix trains r × 8,192 params instead of 16,777,216.

4. The real-world recipe

  • Pick a small open base model. Llama 3.2 1B or Qwen3 1.7B are the usual starting points. Small enough to fine-tune cheaply, smart enough to be worth steering.
  • Build your dataset. Hundreds to thousands of examples of the behavior you want: prompt in, ideal response out. This is the actual work. Garbage in, garbage personality out.
  • Rent a GPU for a few hours. Roughly $2 to $6 per hour. LoRA keeps the memory footprint small enough for a single GPU.
  • Train with LoRA, watch the loss curve (like section 1), and stop when your evals look good, not when the loss hits zero. Zero loss means it memorized your examples.
  • Evaluate on things it hasn't seen. A fine-tune that only works on your training examples is a parrot, not a model.

That's the whole game. No GPU cluster, no PhD, no training from scratch. Just a base model, your examples, and a few dollars of compute.
