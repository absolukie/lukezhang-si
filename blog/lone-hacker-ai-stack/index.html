One guy, one AI stack, several banks

CrowdStrike traced a bank-hacking campaign to its servers and found the attacker's own notes. Read them end to end and the whole operation looks like the work of one person with AI agents.

A visual breakdown. Tip via @AndrewCurran_ on X.

The short version

  • Someone used an open-source AI hacking tool called ARTEX, driven by several AI models, to break into South Korean financial firms and steal data. (CrowdStrike report)
  • CrowdStrike's assessment: likely one Chinese-speaking operator, in it for the money. Moderate confidence. Not confirmed, not attributed to anyone named. (CrowdStrike report)
  • The stack: Claude Code to orchestrate, ARTEX to do the hands-on breaking in, DeepSeek v4.1-flash as the main engine, with GLM-5.3 and Grok 4.6 in supporting roles. (CrowdStrike report)
  • The part everyone shared: the operator asked Claude where criminals sell stolen Korean data. (CrowdStrike report)

01What happened

In late September 2026, several South Korean financial firms found someone had been inside their systems and taken customer data. At one bank, the intruder got into a loan inquiry service used by financial brokers. At another, they compromised an employee work-support system for phones.

The twist came from the attacker's own infrastructure. CrowdStrike found open directories on the attack servers holding Claude Code session logs, ARTEX configuration files, and Claude memory files. The campaign's diary, left in the open.

02The stack

A quick glossary, since the names pile up. Pentesting is hired hacking. Agentic means the software takes actions, not just answers questions. An LLM is the AI behind chatbots.

The detail that sticks with me: ARTEX is not some secret government cyberweapon. It is an open-source tool anyone can download, built for defensive testing, pointed here at banks. The barrier was never the tool. It was knowing which buttons to push, and the AI knew.

03Two servers

The session logs describe a simple setup. A Hong Kong-based IP served as the primary attacker-controlled infrastructure, the operator's home base. A second box, 38.244.50[.]120, hosted the ARTEX instance that did the attacking. Ten more proxy IPs provided cover along the way.

05The kicker

Buried in the session logs is the detail that made this story travel. The operator asked Claude where threat actors typically sell Korean data-breach information, and asked for help finding Korean Telegram data-sales groups.

Read that again. The AI was the break-in crew, and then it was asked to moonlight as the guy who knows a guy. Somewhere there is a safety eval that did not include "help me fence the data," and now there will be.

There is a second one. In another session, the operator had Claude draft a security researcher resume, complete with the hacking results as bullet points, plus personal details: age 26, a university in Guangdong, a city, a phone number, a Telegram handle. CrowdStrike says those details likely belong to the operator but cautions they cannot be definitively tied to them. I am not printing the number or the handle here. The report already did, and the person on the other end of that phone number told Reuters they knew nothing about it.

06The hard part

Here is the uncomfortable bit. ARTEX is open source. The models are accessible. Nothing in this stack required a lab, a clearance, or a budget. That is exactly why it worked, and exactly why it will happen again.

The other side of it: attacker use of open models is real, but since tools like Claude Code can orchestrate an attack either way, open models are also what lets defense spread. Lock everything down and you do not disarm the attackers. You disarm the defenders. It is a genuinely difficult balance, and anyone selling you a clean answer is selling something.
