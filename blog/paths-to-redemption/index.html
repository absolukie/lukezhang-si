Paths to redemption after a permanent ban

October 5, 2026 · gaming · moderation
Verdict: A permanent ban with no way back doesn't reform anyone — it just exiles them. Ludwig's argument, from a Minecraft speedrunning ban: replace permanent conduct bans with long timed bans and an explicit appeal path. Punishment without a route to redemption makes the community worse, not better.

What happened

EDCR, one of the best Minecraft speedrunners in the world — he won last year's MCSR season 11 playoffs — was permanently banned from future playoffs by the Minecraft ranked team. Old Discord DMs from 2023–24 surfaced mid-playoffs; he was 16–17 when he sent them, 19 now. The messages were homophobic, transphobic, and racist — about as bad as it gets.

He was disqualified from the playoffs immediately, denied sending the messages the same day, then posted a public apology two days later. After the playoffs, the ranked team finalized it: a permanent ban. Ludwig agrees the disqualification was the right call. It's the permanence he disputes.

The proposal: a long ban with a real exit ramp

Instead of permanent, make it a long timed ban — one to two years — with a defined appeal at the end. And the path has to be explicit, not vague: this is the path — in one to two years you can appeal if you still want to compete.

The appeal isn't automatic. He has to ask for it, and reviewers look for evidence of growth: how he's changed, what the people around him say, how he conducts himself in the community. Not enough change? Extend another year, appeal again. There's always a route back, but never a free one.

And honesty is priced in. EDCR lied to the mods at first, then apologized — and only engaged when directly prompted. Ludwig moved his own number from 12–18 months to 2 years specifically because of the lie. Lying extends the sentence.

Why permanence backfires

Sun Tzu: give your enemy an escape route. When there's no path back, the punished don't reform — they get ostracized, stay bad, and form their own bitter alt-communities that poison the main one. Ludwig saw exactly this in the Melee scene. Reviewing appeals costs a few hours over the years; the upside is a person who might actually change. Net benefit.

Skill buys nothing, and cheating is a different crime

Three of the top ~10 players are reportedly banned — being good at the game earns no leniency, and Ludwig is clear that other banned players deserve the same appeal policy. Cheating bans stay in their own category: those exist as deterrence, a warning to everyone else. Conduct is where rehabilitation can actually work.

The proof it can work is Tyler1 — wildly toxic, banned, then a genuine redemption arc into becoming the face of the game. Not one-to-one: EDCR's messages were much worse than anything Tyler1 said in-game, so the ban stays long. But the arc is possible.

Punishment vs. reformation

This is the prison-reform analogy, and Ludwig makes it outright: do we want punishment or reformation? Online communities default to "rot in hell" with zero grace; in person, we're far more willing to forgive. He quotes Marcus Aurelius — "When somebody is a fool, they don't know that they're a fool" — you don't have to suffer fools, but giving someone an opening to stop being one beats assuming bad people are born that way.

The appeal test is practical, not theatrical: is he someone worth having around now? Judged by how he operates today and what the people around him say — not by forgiveness theater.

Why it stuck with me

The idea is older than Minecraft: no one is beyond restoration while they're still willing to change. Grace with a process — not cheap, not absent. A ban that says "show us who you've become in two years" does more work than one that says "never."
