SPF 30 vs 50 vs 60:
the number is the least interesting part

Verdict: SPF 30 is the sweet spot for daily use. SPF 50 earns its keep on beach days and long exposure. SPF 60 over 50 is mostly marketing. And how much you apply beats all three numbers — four interactive visualizers below, the math is real.

4 interactive visualizers real formulas verdict-first

October 5, 2026 · skincare · sunscreen · ← all posts

I was picking a morning sunscreen and fell down this rabbit hole. The SPF number on the bottle looks like a score — bigger must be better, right? The math says something more interesting: the difference between 30 and 50 is real, the difference between 50 and 60 is nearly nothing, and the thing that actually decides your protection isn't on the label at all.

The definitionWhat SPF actually measures

SPF is a ratio: the UVB dose needed to redden your skin with sunscreen divided by the dose needed without it. SPF 30 means, under lab conditions, it takes 30 times more UVB to redden protected skin.

Two things the number doesn't tell you:

It's UVB-only. SPF measures protection against UVB — the burning rays. UVA rays are the aging ones, and they need the words "broad spectrum" on the label. The number says nothing about UVA. Always buy broad spectrum.

It's a lab number. SPF is measured at 2 mg/cm² of product — about a shot glass (1 oz) for a full body, a nickel-sized dollop for your face. Almost nobody applies that much. We'll come back to this, because it's the whole game.

The mathWhat each number actually blocks

The percentage of UVB blocked is (SPF − 1) / SPF. Run the numbers and the marketing falls apart:

Notice the trick: the interesting column isn't "blocked" — it's "gets through." Going from 30 to 50 cuts the transmitted UVB roughly in half (3.3% → 2.0%). Going from 50 to 60 shaves off 0.3 points. That's the whole story of diminishing returns.

Diminishing returnsEach step up buys less

Think of it in photons. Out of 100 UVB photons hitting your skin, SPF 30 lets ~3 through, SPF 50 lets ~2 through, SPF 60 lets ~1.7 through. The first upgrade halves your exposure. The second is a rounding error wearing a bigger number.

The real variableHow much you actually apply

Here's the part nobody puts on the bottle. Lab SPF is measured at 2 mg/cm². Studies consistently find most people apply a quarter to a half of that — a pea-sized dab instead of a nickel-sized dollop, a thin smear instead of a shot glass.

Protection falls off fast when you under-apply. A common rough model: effective SPF ≈ labeled SPF ^ (fraction applied). It's an approximation, not a lab measurement — but it shows the shape of the problem. Half a coat of SPF 30 doesn't give you SPF 15. It gives you roughly SPF 5.5.

This is why "apply more beats a bigger number" isn't a slogan, it's arithmetic. A full coat of SPF 30 (effective 30) lets through about 4× less UVB than a half coat of SPF 60 (effective ~7.7). The other ruleReapplication beats the number

No sunscreen survives the day on one coat. The protective film breaks down in UV, rubs off on clothes and towels, and mixes with sweat. The rule is every 2 hours, and immediately after swimming or heavy sweating — regardless of what number is on the bottle.

Don't trust these numbers past 2 hours.

"SPF 60 × 10 minutes = 10 hours" is lab arithmetic on a perfect, undisturbed film. In real life the film is never perfect and never undisturbed. Reapply every 2 hours no matter what the bottle promises — the clock that matters is the reapplication clock, not the SPF clock.

The verdictWhat to actually buy

Daily / office days → SPF 30.

Applied at the full amount and reapplied if you're out midday, it's the sweet spot. I fell down this rabbit hole picking a morning sunscreen — SPF 30 is the number I'd default to for everyday use.

Beach / hiking / long exposure → SPF 50.

When you're in direct sun for hours, halving the transmitted UVB (3.3% → 2.0%) is worth paying for. I grabbed the 3 oz travel size for exactly these days.

Burn-prone or very fair skin → SPF 50.

Smaller margin for error means the extra headroom matters.

SPF 60 over 50 → skip it.

A 0.3-point gain that vanishes the moment you apply slightly less than the lab amount. It's a bigger number on the bottle, not meaningfully more protection on your face.

And the two rules that outrank every number: broad spectrum, always (the SPF number says nothing about UVA aging rays), and the best sunscreen is the one you'll actually reapply. A cheap SPF 30 you wear every day beats an SPF 60 that lives in a drawer.
