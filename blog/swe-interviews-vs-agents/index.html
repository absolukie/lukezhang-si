Hire the Reviewer, Not the Typer

Lauren Tan proposed cutting software interviews to two rounds: system design, and building something real with coding agents. The industry is already halfway there. And the data says the new thing being tested is not output. It is judgment.

From the post by @poteto, October 9, 2026. Every number below links to its primary source.

01The two-round proposal

Lauren Tan, who works on Grok at SpaceXAI and previously on the React compiler core team and at Cursor, posted that SWE interviews could collapse to two technical rounds. Round one: system design, testing whether a candidate can articulate ideas and actually engineer something. Round two: an onsite project where the candidate builds a real thing with coding agents, testing whether they can turn intent into high-quality outcomes. Everything else interviews used to ask about, she says, is no longer necessary.

Her follow-up reply added a bonus criterion: show me your Factorio or Satisfactory save file. And a reply from @singhai proposed a third round: hand candidates existing AI-generated projects and ask them to identify and fix the issues.

She was quote-posting Geoffrey Huntley, who argues that AI use in software engineering is no longer optional, and that teams too busy with their normal job to experiment with AI are preparing to be replaced. His hiring advice sorts candidates into buckets. Unacceptable: a poor attitude toward AI, deceptive AI use in interviews, no tangible AI experience. Acceptable: can whiteboard how a coding agent works under the hood. Ideal: a portfolio of AI side projects. His core hiring question is the right one: how do you identify skilled operators now that LLMs can smash LeetCode?

02The industry is already moving

The industry is split, not converged, but the direction is obvious:

  • Meta (Oct 2025): replaced one of its two coding rounds with a 60-minute AI-enabled round on CoderPad. Candidates get an existing codebase and build a feature or fix bugs. The other coding round stays no-AI.
  • Google (H2 2026 pilot, US, junior to mid-level): candidates may use Gemini in a code comprehension round, reading, debugging, and optimizing existing code. Internal framing is human-led, AI-assisted. Pichai said in April 2026 that 75% of new code at Google is AI-generated and engineer-approved, up from 50% the prior fall.
  • Amazon: still no-AI. Online assessments log browser activity, restrict copy-paste, and block logins, which rules out most AI tooling.
  • Canva and Shopify: AI tools allowed in parts of their interviews.
  • Anthropic: explicitly bans AI tools during evaluations.
  • HackerRank Chakra (Oct 2026): an AI interviewer where the candidate works a real repo with an AI assistant in the canvas, with follow-up questions drawn from the work context. HackerRank reports suspicious-activity flags 70 to 80% lower than in comparable traditional assessments, and says open AI access reduces the incentive to secretly use outside tools.

Meanwhile, 43% of technical assessments still include LeetCode-style algorithmic questions, per CoderPad's 2026 report. The old world has not left the building. It is just sharing the stage.

03The cheating paradox

Here is the finding that should end the debate about banning AI in interviews. HackerRank's Chakra, the AI interviewer, reports suspicious-activity flags 70 to 80% lower than comparable traditional assessments. Their explanation: when candidates are allowed to use AI openly, the incentive to secretly use outside tools collapses.

04The Factorio save file

The most fun idea in the thread is also the most serious: ask for the Factorio save file. A save is unfakeable in the relevant sense. Science per minute, factory layout, and bottleneck fixes are checkable artifacts of sustained systems work, unlike a take-home that an agent can now generate in an afternoon.

Hacker News commenters have long argued you can learn to be an effective distributed-systems engineer just by playing Factorio: concurrency patterns, backpressure, buffering, and load balancing, all reinvented while scaling a resource-constrained system. The community benchmark is science per minute, with novices at 0 to 30, intermediates at 30 to 200, and advanced players above 200. Small inefficiencies ripple into crippling bottlenecks, which is exactly the production-debugging muscle.

It has even been tried as an interview. In 2021, Erik McClure ran Factorio as a cooperative technical interview, on the theory that it exposes how candidates think while scaling a production line as a team. The catch: each interview took an estimated 8 to 20 hours. That is why it never caught on. But as a bonus signal attached to a portfolio, the save file is the resume: sustained systems work, not solved puzzles.

05The steelman: why algorithms rounds might stay

The case against poteto's proposal deserves a fair hearing, because the strongest objections are not nostalgia:

  • The AI round is a fundamentals test in disguise. You cannot catch a subtly broken binary search if you do not know what a correct one looks like. You cannot challenge a bad complexity claim if Big-O is vibes to you. The model produces plausible code; plausible only converts to correct through a reviewer who knows things.
  • Standardization and fairness. Algorithmic problems are artificial but comparable across candidates. Real-world discussions depend heavily on interviewer skill and the candidate's prior exposure. Juniors with strong problem-solving ability but no large-system experience still need a way to show potential.
  • Weak signal, but a legible one. LeetCode correlates weakly with job performance and rewards memorization, which is an argument for reform, not necessarily removal.
  • The false-confidence risk. Jack Dorsey warned that AI-assisted hiring may give false confidence in weak engineers; Casey Muratori warns that vibecoding erodes core skills without architecture understanding.
  • Even the reformers keep the fundamentals. The people arguing LeetCode interviews measure the wrong thing still say: learn algorithms for the work, not the interview. Study them to become a better engineer, not just to pass interviews.

The honest synthesis: poteto's two rounds test judgment and production, but judgment presupposes the fundamentals the algorithms round was imperfectly measuring. The debate is not whether fundamentals matter. It is whether producing code from scratch under time pressure is still the right way to measure them, now that the job is reviewing agent output.

The verdict

  • The interview is becoming a trust test: can this person judge machine output?
  • 65.9% of developers use the tools; 6.6% trust them with an important decision. That gap is the job now.
  • Banning AI in interviews does not prevent AI use. It prevents honest AI use.
  • The fundamentals still matter, but typing them from memory under time pressure measures the wrong half of the new job.
  • The new whiteboard is a repo full of agent-written code. The only question that matters: would you ship it?
