Why Richard gets shortened to Dick

October 4, 2026 · language · etymology

Verdict: Richard got chopped to Rick because in the 1200s everything was written by hand, and long names cost time. Then medieval England's rhyming-nickname habit turned Rick into Dick — the same game that turned William into Will into Bill, and Robert into Rob into Bob. The dirty meaning of "dick" arrived centuries later and has nothing to do with the nickname. It just killed it.

Step one: shorten it

Richard is Old Germanic — ric (ruler, power) + hard (brave, strong), roughly "hard ruler." After the Norman conquest it became one of the most common names in England, and scribes writing everything by hand had no patience for eight letters. So Richard became Ric, Rich, and Rick. This is the normal hypocorism step — the affectionate shortening every name gets. Nothing weird yet.

Step two: rhyme it

Here's the part that looks like madness. Medieval English speakers had a fashion for rhyming nicknames: take the shortened name, swap the first consonant, and see what sticks. Rick became Dick — and also Hick, which didn't survive. Nobody was trying to make sense. It was a nickname game, the same one that turned Will into Bill and Rob into Bob.

It helped that England had a shortage of first names. When half the village is named Richard or William, you need a lot of nicknames just to tell people apart. The rhyming variants were practical before they were traditions.

Dick isn't even the weird one

English nicknames are full of these chains once you look: Edward → Ed → Ned (and Ted), Henry → Hank, James → Jim, Margaret → Peggy, Mary → Polly, Ann → Nancy. Dick only stands out because the other meaning moved in later and made everyone giggle. William got lucky — "Bill" never picked up a second meaning.

The dirty meaning came centuries later

This is the part most explanations skip: the timeline. Dick-as-nickname dates to the 13th century. Dick-as-jerk doesn't appear for a few hundred years after that. Dick-as-penis shows up in the 1890s, as British army slang. The nickname is older than both meanings by an enormous margin — the association is pure coincidence, and it's what eventually destroyed the name. Dick fell out of the top 1,000 US baby names by 1969. Richard is fine; nobody names their kid Dick anymore.

The takeaway

Richard → Rick → Dick is a two-step process: shorten for convenience, then rhyme for fun. It's the same two steps behind Bill, Bob, Ned, and Peggy. The only reason Dick sounds absurd today is that a slang term from the 1890s colonized it. Strip that away and it's as ordinary as Bob.
