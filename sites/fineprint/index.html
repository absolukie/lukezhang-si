Know the traps before you sign

The ten clauses warranty companies use to deny claims, in plain English. These are the exact patterns FinePrint scans for.

"Wear and tear" exclusion

The number-one denial phrase in the industry. Almost any mechanical failure can be called wear and tear, and the company decides what counts.

Watch for: "wear and tear", "normal wear"

"Sole discretion" denials

The contract lets the company decide claims however it wants. Combined with any exclusion, denials are effectively unappealable.

Watch for: "sole discretion", "at our discretion"

Binding arbitration

You give up your right to sue. Disputes go to a private arbitrator, a process the company uses constantly and you will use once.

Watch for: "binding arbitration"

Class action waiver

You cannot join other customers in a group lawsuit, even if the company systematically denies valid claims.

Watch for: "waive any right to participate in a class action"

Maintenance records required

The number-one real-world denial trigger. One missing oil-change receipt can void a claim, regardless of what actually broke.

Watch for: "maintenance records", "proof of maintenance"

Seals and gaskets excluded

A $20 seal fails and cooks a $4,000 transmission. The seal is excluded while the transmission is "covered". One of the oldest traps in the book.

Watch for: "seals and gaskets"

Aggregate claim cap

A hard ceiling on everything the company will ever pay you. Divide the cap by the contract price: if one repair nearly exhausts it, you are buying very little.

Watch for: "aggregate limit", "shall not exceed $X"

Prior authorization required

No work starts until the company approves it, which can take days. Your car sits at the shop while you wait.

Watch for: "prior authorization", "pre-authorization"

Waiting period

Nothing is covered for the first 30 days or 1,000 miles. If something breaks on day 29, you pay.

Watch for: "coverage begins 30 days"

Betterment charges

The company deducts "betterment" or depreciation from your payout, so an approved $3,000 repair can arrive as a much smaller check.

Watch for: "betterment", "depreciation"

Questions

Straight answers.

Is this legal advice?
No. FinePrint is pattern matching against 30+ known warranty-trap phrases, explained in plain English. It will miss cleverly worded traps and occasionally flag harmless text. If the score is low, take the contract to a mechanic you trust or your state attorney general's consumer protection office.
How does the detection work?
The demo scans your contract text for known trap patterns (exclusion language, maintenance requirements, claim caps, arbitration clauses) and scores what it finds. No AI, no sign-in. For sync, the app creates an anonymous device key in this browser. It is not an account, but anyone holding the key can read your synced records, so treat it like a password. Device sync is on by default: copies of your analyses are stored on our server so they follow you to your other devices.
Does my contract leave my device?
The scan itself runs in your browser, but device sync is on by default: copies of your analyses are stored on our server so they follow you to your other devices. Turn sync off to keep everything on this device only.
What about PDF contracts?
Copy the text out of the PDF and paste it in, or save as .txt and upload. Direct PDF parsing is on the roadmap.
What if it finds nothing?
That means none of the known patterns matched, not that the contract is safe. Clever drafting can dodge pattern matching. Always read the exclusions section yourself.
