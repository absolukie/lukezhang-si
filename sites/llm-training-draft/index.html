How an LLM Learns, Step by Step

October 7, 2026 · llms · machine learning

Training a language model is one game played trillions of times: cover up the next word, guess it, measure how surprised you were, and adjust. Everything else, tokens, attention, gradient descent, RLHF, is machinery that makes that game work at scale.

People talk about training like it is magic or like it is just math. It is neither. It is a very large, very patient student doing fill-in-the-blank quizzes, checking the answer key, and nudging its brain a tiny bit after every single question. Do that a few trillion times and the guessing starts to look like thinking.

This post walks through every step, and every step has something you can touch. Type into the tokenizer. Poke the attention weights. Break gradient descent with a bad learning rate on purpose. By the end you will have run a miniature version of the whole pipeline in your browser.

1. The model never sees words. It sees tokens.

Before training starts, all text gets chopped into pieces called tokens. A token can be a whole common word ("the"), a chunk of a word ("ing"), or a single character. The model has a fixed vocabulary, usually 50,000 to 200,000 tokens, and everything it ever reads or writes is built from those pieces.

Why not just use words? English has over a million of them, and new ones appear daily. Tokens are the compromise: common words get their own token, rare words get split into pieces. The splits are learned from data by an algorithm called BPE (byte pair encoding): start with single characters, then repeatedly merge the most common adjacent pair, until the vocabulary is full.

Two details that matter later. First, every token becomes an ID number, and the model only ever works with those numbers. Second, token counts are the currency of the whole field: context windows, pricing, and training budgets are all measured in tokens, not words. A rough rule: 1 token is about 3/4 of an English word.

2. Tokens become lists of numbers

A token ID is just a label. The model needs something it can do math on, so each token gets an embedding: a list of a few hundred or thousand numbers, its coordinates in meaning-space. These numbers start random. Training moves them around until words used in similar ways end up near each other.

3. Tokens look at each other: attention

Here is the famous part. A word's meaning depends on context, so before the model decides anything, every token gets to look at every other token and pull in information. That is attention: for each token, a set of weights saying how much each other token matters right now.

The classic example: "The animal did not cross the street because it was too tired." What does "it" refer to? The model learns to point "it" back at "animal". Tap a word in the sentence and see where its attention goes. These weights are illustrative, but the mechanism is exactly the real one: weighted mixing, recomputed fresh for every word in every sentence.

Real models run many attention heads in parallel, each learning a different kind of relationship: grammar, coreference, position, and patterns with no English name. This demo shows one head's job.

One more detail: attention is why these models are called transformers. The architecture is stacks of identical blocks, and each block is mostly "attention, then a small neural net, repeated." A big model just stacks that block 100 times.

4. The only game: predict the next token

After attention and the small neural nets (called MLPs) have done their mixing, the model outputs a score for every token in its vocabulary: how likely is each one to come next? Those scores are called logits, and they get squashed into probabilities that sum to 1 by a function called softmax.

Training is one repeated quiz: hide the next token, make the model guess, then score it. The score is the loss, and for this game it has a beautifully simple form called cross-entropy: negative log of the probability the model gave to the correct answer. Gave the truth 72%? Loss is small. Gave it 6%? Loss is large. The model's whole education is "be less surprised, on average, over trillions of quizzes."

The model does not output probabilities. It outputs raw scores, called logits. Softmax turns raw scores into probabilities that add to 100%: bigger score, bigger slice. Drag the scores and watch.

Which word is actually correct?

Loss: how much probability went to the right word ?

Softmax: each probability = e^(score) / sum of e^(score) over all words. Subtracting the biggest score first keeps the exponentials small; that is the log-sum-exp trick behind log-softmax. Log space keeps the math stable: computers add logs safely, but multiplying thousands of tiny probabilities would round to zero. Loss = -log(probability of the correct word), in nats.

5. Learning is rolling downhill

So the model gets a loss score on every quiz. How does it improve? Every weight in the model gets a tiny adjustment in the direction that would have lowered the loss. Computing those directions for millions of weights at once is backpropagation: the chain rule from calculus, applied mechanically, layer by layer, backwards through the network.

Then comes gradient descent: move each weight a little bit downhill. The size of the step is the learning rate, and picking it is the oldest drama in machine learning. Too small and training crawls. Too big and the model bounces around or blows up entirely. Try it below. This is a one-weight model on a simple valley, but the rule is identical in 100 billion dimensions.

Real training does not update after every quiz. It averages the gradient over a batch, thousands of quizzes at once, then takes one step. Smoother signal, and GPUs love doing the same math on many examples in parallel.

6. Why scale wins

Here is the uncomfortable truth the field learned around 2020: the same algorithm, just bigger, keeps getting smarter. Bigger model, more data, more compute. The scaling laws say loss falls predictably as you scale any of the three, which is why training runs now cost tens of millions of dollars.

The sharpest version of this is Chinchilla (DeepMind, 2022). For a fixed compute budget, the best results come from splitting it evenly: half on a bigger model, half on more training data. The rule of thumb that fell out: train on about 20 tokens per parameter. Drag the slider and feel the numbers.

8. Picking the word: temperature and top-p

Training is over. Now you ask a question and the model must actually choose a token. It has probabilities, not a decision procedure, so generation is sampling: roll a weighted die, append the word, repeat. Two knobs control the personality of that die.

Temperature sharpens or flattens the distribution before sampling. Near zero, the top choice always wins: boring, safe, deterministic. High, and unlikely words start winning: creative, weird, unhinged. Top-p (nucleus sampling) chops off the long tail entirely: only sample from the smallest set of words whose probabilities add up to p.

It never understands. It just gets less surprised, trillions of times, until the guessing looks like thinking.
